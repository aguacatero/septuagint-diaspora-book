2. Alternative Explanations the Author Considers
Two Consecutive Soteriological Stages (Faith then Confession):
Considered View: Held by Charles Hodge, Sanday & Headlam, William Barclay, James Montgomery Boice, and Frédéric Godet, who argue that faith in the heart is insufficient on its own and must be followed consecutively by outward verbal confession to achieve final, future salvation (σωθήσεται, cf. Rom 13:11) [46–48].
Oliver's Assessment: Oliver rejects this strict chronological reading, stating that the pericope does not depict two sequential steps in justification, but rather informs readers about the dynamic consequences of their actions within the Christian life
23
.
"Two Sides of the Same Coin" / Synonymous Parallelism:
Considered View: Held by Anders Nygren, Douglas Moo, G. Aho, Colin Kruse, Akio Ito, James Dunn, and Alan F. Johnson, who argue that faith and confession are two aspects of a single saving action, functioning as a parallelism where "to believe is to confess" [49–50].
Oliver's Assessment: Oliver rejects this view, noting that nothing in the text indicates a poetic synonymous parallelism; on the contrary, Paul explicitly differentiates the anatomical seat ("heart" vs. "mouth") and assigns distinct theological results ("righteousness" vs. "salvation") to each [50–51].
Subjective / Mere General Universality in Isaiah 28:16:
Considered View: Oliver considers G. Aho's postulation that Isaiah 28:16 in Romans 10:11 simply stipulates that the way of faith is open to all [21–22].
Oliver's Assessment: Oliver cautions that this may be a "too subjective reading" if divorced from Paul's specific lexical link between καταισχύνω and ἐπαισχύνομαι in Romans 1:16 [17, 21–22].
Calling as a Purely Private Soul Confession:
Considered View: H. A. Ironside's view that Paul has the "soul's confession to God Himself" in mind rather than public confession before human beings

Oliver's Assessment: Oliver refutes this by citing Greek lexicography (F. W. Danker, Louw & Nida) showing that ὁμολογέω denotes an open, public profession of allegiance

Structural Paraphrase: Early Christian Baptismal Liturgical Origin: Although Oliver mentions in footnote 14 that ἐὰν ὁμολογήσῃς... κύριον Ἰησοῦν is regarded by Ulrich Wilckens and Eduard Lohse as a traditional early Christian baptismal confession formula [40–41], he does not address whether this pre-Pauline baptismal setting fully explains why confession and salvation are conjoined here, treating it instead as Paul's own unique, ad hoc prophetic creation.

4. What Evidence Would Weaken or Falsify the Author's Conclusion
Disproof of Rhetorical/Chiastic Intent: Oliver acknowledges that if the structural arrangement of Romans 10:9–10 were proven to be an ordinary Hebrew poetic parallelism (as argued by Ito, Dunn, and Johnson), the two statements would simply be synonymous
27
. This would falsify Oliver's claim that Paul intentionally separated heart/faith from mouth/confession to assign them distinct functions [50–51].
Absence of Prophetic-Apostolic Intent in Romans 10:14–15: Oliver’s thesis that Romans 10:5–13 emphasizes the prophetic duty of the individual believer to proclaim the gospel depends on linking verse 13 directly to the missionary preacher chain in verses 14–15 [66–67, 72–73]. If historical or exegetical evidence demonstrated that verses 14–15 refer strictly to authorized apostolic mission heralds (such as Paul himself) and not to the soteriological obligations of ordinary believers, Oliver's conclusion—that every believer must "do something-being-said" in order to receive salvation—would be substantially weakened [29, 69, 72–73].
