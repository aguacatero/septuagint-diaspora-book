1. Hebrew Wording and Grammatical Ambiguity of Deut 21:23
Printed Page Numbers: Pages 152–153
3
5
.
Hebrew Text: The original text reads כִּי קִלְלַת אֱלֹהִים תָּלוּי ("for (the) curse of God (is) he that is hanged")
3
.
Grammatical Ambiguity: Lightfoot explains that "the ambiguity arises out of the construction of אֱלֹהִים, since the case attached to קִלְלַת may denote either the person who pronounces the curse... or the person against whom the curse is pronounced... in other words, it represents either a subjective or an objective genitive"
3
.
Two Interpretations:
Subjective Genitive: "He that is hanged is accursed in the sight of God" (taking God as the one pronouncing the curse)
3
.
Objective Genitive: "He that hangeth is a contempt of, a reproach or insult to God" (taking God as the object against whom the insult/reproach is directed)
3
.
2. Translation in Ancient Versions
Printed Page Numbers: Pages 152–153
3
unfold_less
5
6
.
Septuagint (LXX): Renders the clause as κεκατηραμένος ὑπὸ τοῦ Θεοῦ ("accursed in the sight of God"), adopting the subjective genitive
3
.
Aquila & Theodotion: Rendered the text as κατάρα Θεοῦ κρεμάμενος, taking Θεοῦ as an objective genitive ("an insult/contempt to God")
6
.
Symmachus: Rendered the clause in Latin as "quia propter blasphemiam Dei suspensus est" ("because he was hanged for blasphemy against God")
6
.
Older Targum (Onkelos): Rendered paraphrastically as "Since for what he sinned before God he was hanged" (אֲרֵי עַל דַּחֲטָא קֳדָם אֱלָהָא אִצְטְלִיב), taking God as the object of the offense
5
6
.
Targum of Pseudo-Jonathan: Adds the explanatory clause "unless his sins have occasioned it to him" (קִילוּנָא קֳדָם אֱלָהָא לְמִצְלוֹב גְּבַר), which Lightfoot notes is "a curious contrast to the interpretation of the older Targum" and possibly "aimed at Christianity"
5
6
.
Peshitta Syriac: Follows the objective rendering, translated as "Because whosoever blasphemeth God shall be hanged"
5
.
3. Patristic Evidence and Jewish-Christian Controversy
Printed Page Numbers: Pages 60, 152–153
3
unfold_less
5
6
7
.
Context: Lightfoot notes that "this passage occupied an important place in the early controversies between the Christians and the Jews"
3
. In synagogue debates, Paul and early apologists repeatedly faced the charge: "'accursed of God,' or 'an insult to God,' or 'a blasphemer of God, is he that is hanged on the tree'"
5
.
Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho): In Dial. c. Tryph. cc. 95, 96, Justin refutes Jewish objections based on Deut 21:23, citing the text exactly as Paul does to demonstrate that Christ bore the curse on behalf of all humanity
5
7
.
Ariston of Pella (Dispute of Jason and Papiscus): Cited by Jerome as recording a Jewish interlocutor who brought forward Deut 21:23 in the form λοιδορία Θεοῦ ὁ κρεμάμενος ("a reviling of God")
5
6
.
Tertullian (Adversus Judaeos): In Adv. Jud. § 10, Tertullian meets and refutes the Jewish inference drawn from Deuteronomy 21:23
5
.
Jerome (Commentary on Galatians): Records that in his day "the same argument was brought by the Jews against the leading fact on which the faith of a Christian rests"
5
. Jerome also quotes the Ebionite leader as interpreting the words as ὕβρις Θεοῦ ὁ κρεμάμενος ("an injury/reproach to God")
6
.
Evagrius (Altercatio inter Theophilum Christianum et Simonem Judaeum): Writing c. 430 A.D., Evagrius's dialogue shows that "later literature shows that Christ crucified did not cease to be 'to the Jews a stumblingblock'"
5
.
4. Omission of 'by God' (ὑπὸ Θεοῦ) in Galatians 3:13
Printed Page Numbers: Pages 139–140
1
2
.
Citation Comparison: In Galatians 3:13, Paul cites Deut 21:23 as ἐπικατάρατος πᾶς ὁ κρεμάμενος ἐπὶ ξύλου ("Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree"), omitting the words ὑπὸ Θεοῦ found in the Septuagint (κεκατηραμένος ὑπὸ Θεοῦ)
1
2
.
Reason for Omission: Lightfoot explains that "our Lord had died the death of the worst malefactors: He had undergone that punishment, which under the law betokened the curse of God. So far He had become κατάρα. But He was in no literal sense κατάρατος ὑπὸ Θεοῦ, and St Paul instinctively omits those words which do not strictly apply, and which, if added, would have required some qualification"
2
.
Vicarious Nature of the Curse: Christ was not personally accursed by God as a sinner; rather, "the victim is regarded as bearing the sins of those for whom atonement is made. The curse is transferred from them to it. It becomes in a certain sense the impersonation of the sin and of the curse" (γενόμενος ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν κατάρα)
1
.
5. Original Meaning of Deuteronomy vs. Application to Crucifixion
Printed Page Numbers: Page 154
4
.
Original Reference: Lightfoot states that "the passage in Deuteronomy, it is true, does not refer directly to crucifixion as a means of execution, but to impaling bodies after death"
4
.
Philo's Misinterpretation: Lightfoot corrects the claim that Philo viewed Deuteronomy 21:23 as execution by impalement: "Philo says, that Moses would have put such malefactors to death ten thousand times over... he adds another penalty, ordering murderers to be gibbeted (τιμωρίαν ἄλλην προσδιατάττεται...)"
4
.
Legitimacy of Paul's Application: "For though this passage did not contemplate death by crucifixion, the application was quite legitimate. It was the hanging, not the death, that brought ignominy on the sufferer and defilement on the land"
4
.
Jewish and Christian Transmission:
The Chaldee paraphrase of Deuteronomy uses the same word (צְלַב) that the Peshitta Syriac uses to describe Christ's crucifixion in Galatians 3:1
4
.
Later Rabbinic writers referred to Jesus by the reproachful term תָּלוּי ("the gibbeted one"), drawn directly from the text of Deuteronomy 21:23
4
.
Lightfoot concludes: "No Jew would have questioned the propriety of St Paul's application of the text to our Lord. The curse pronounced in the law was interpreted and strengthened by the national sentiment"
4
.
