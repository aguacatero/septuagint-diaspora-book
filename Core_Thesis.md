# Septuagint Diaspora Book — Core Thesis & Boundaries

## Working Title
*Shaped by the Septuagint: Reassessing the Hebrew Influence (or Lack Thereof) on the New Testament*

## Layered Thesis (Strongest Defensible Position)
1. **Greek Jewish Scripture was a major—and often dominant—source for NT argumentation.** The LXX (Greek Septuagint) was the primary Scripture for Hellenistic Jewish diaspora communities and shaped much of the NT's scriptural reasoning. Hebrew/Aramaic traditions remain possible but must be demonstrated case by case for each citation or narrative element.

2. **Historical-Jesus question bracketed.** The textual-linguistic argument stands independently: the LXX's dominance in NT citations is a linguistic/sociolinguistic fact regardless of whether a minimal historical Jesus existed. Neither assumed nor denied in the core argument.

3. **Primary corpus:** Undisputed Pauline corpus + Mark + Matthew + Luke–Acts. Patristic witnesses (Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus) used for earliest Christian language/context, not as independent historical anchors.

4. **Evidence protocol:** Every claim must have:
   - Primary text reference (with chapter/verse, critical edition)
   - Date/attestation layer
   - Exact wording in both LXX and Masoretic Text (MT) / Hebrew Vorlage
   - Explicit comparison of which textual tradition the NT author appears to follow
   - Mainstream counter-argument (with citation)
   - Counter-counter showing why LXX dominance still makes sense
   - Confidence level and falsifiability note

5. **Mythic parallels:** Separate appendix (or second project). Current work: explicitly labeled speculative, not integrated into main argument's evidentiary chain.

## What's IN the Core Argument

### Systematic LXX-vs-MT Comparison
- Every major OT citation in Mark, Paul, and the Gospel narrative
- Precise textual collation: Göttingen LXX vs. BHS for each
- Document: which reading the NT author appears to follow; why; what Hebrew alternative exists

### Diaspora Sociolinguistic Evidence
- Greek as lingua franca in diaspora synagogues (Alexandria, Antioch, Rome)
- Inscriptions/papyri showing Greek dominance in public/religious life
- Hebrew literacy confined to specialized priestly/scholarly circles; broader laity encountered Scripture in Greek
- Earliest Christian communities: Greek-speaking, LXX-oriented

### Earliest Christian References (as language/context evidence, not historical proof)
- Pliny the Younger's letter: shows Greek-speaking Christian communities with defined creed; NOT a statement on their Hebrew literacy
- Ignatius of Antioch: letters steeped in LXX language; addresses Greek-speaking churches
- Acts: Greek composition; depicts diaspora Jewish communities reading Scripture in Greek
- Papyrus 46 (Chester Beatty): earliest Pauline manuscript; contains LXX citations; confirms Greek-speaking communities valued LXX quotations

### Non-Christian Sources as Language Evidence (not independent historical attestation)
- Josephus: Greek-language historiography; "brother of Jesus" passage discussed with arguments for interpolation; even if authentic, reflects Greek literary conventions of the genre
- Tacitus: Latin author reporting on Greek-speaking movement; "Christus" execution under Pilate reflects Roman awareness of Christian movement but does not provide Hebrew/Aramaic source evidence
- These are cited as *contextual* evidence of how the movement was perceived in Greek/Latin literary culture, not as independent historical anchors.

### Computational/Stylometric Potential (future appendix)
- Stylometric comparison of LXX-specific vocabulary frequency in NT vs. typical Hellenistic Greek
- Open-access dataset proposal for future testing

## What's EXPLICITLY OUT of the Core Argument

- **No claim that Hebrew/Aramaic was *entirely absent***. The argument is about *relative dominance* and *probable authorial practice*.
- **No assertion that the Gospels were *invented* from whole cloth**. The claim is that their *scriptural exegesis* relies on the LXX.
- **No definitive historicity position**. The historical-Jesus question is bracketed; the book addresses textual provenance, not archaeological historicity.
- **No sweeping mythicist conclusions**. Mythic parallels (Osiris, Asclepius, etc.) are labeled speculative in a separate appendix; they do not form part of the core LXX-dependence argument.
- **No claim that Paul "didn't know Hebrew."** The thesis is that Paul (and other NT authors) *treated the LXX as authoritative Scripture* and built their arguments on its phrasing, regardless of their possible Hebrew competence. The evidence shows LXX as the *functional* text they appealed to.

## Appendix C — Deep-Dive Reinforcement: 1st-Century Climate & Literary Patterns (Bracketed, Not Core)

Purpose: Build a body of circumstantial and literary evidence that reinforces the LXX-dependence thesis by showing the Greek-speaking Jewish diaspora's scriptural culture, the literary conventions of the Gospels, and the historical context of 1st-century communities.

### 1. LXX vs MT — Per Gospel Evidence (Mark / Matthew / Luke / John)
For each canonical gospel, document:
- Every major OT citation; LXX wording vs MT/Hebrew Vorlage; which tradition the NT author appears to follow.
- Confidence rating (high/medium/low) and falsifier (what discovery would undermine).
- Note: Q source removed; comparison is between the gospel text and the LXX/MT critical editions directly.

### 2. 1st-Century Climate — Historical / Contextual Evidence
- **Dura-Europos synagogue inscriptions** (3rd-c., but showing Greek dominance; note dating limitation).
- **Pliny the Younger's letter to Trajan** (c. 112 CE): describes Greek-speaking Christian communities worshiping Christus; not proof of Jesus' historicity, but evidence of Greek-language religious practice.
- **Ignatius of Antioch** (early 2nd-c.): letters steeped in LXX language, addressing Greek-speaking churches.
- **Acts**: depicts diaspora Jewish communities reading Scripture in Greek; Greek composition.
- **Papyrus 46 (Chester Beatty)**: earliest Pauline manuscript with LXX citations.
- **Didache / Shepherd of Hermas**: LXX-based Eucharist prayers and visions.

### 3. Literary / Circumstantial Evidence
- **Midrashic expansions** in Matthew: how LXX phrasing is incorporated into narrative structures.
- **Fulfillment formulas** as evidence of deliberate scriptural selection from LXX texts.
- **Greek idiom and vocabulary patterns** distinct from Hebrew syntax, reinforcing the thesis of Greek-language composition.
- **Non-canonical early gospels (bracketed)**: Gospel of Thomas (Coptic, 2nd-c., not 1st-c., no direct LXX dependence shown); Gospel of Peter; Gospel of Mary; Papyrus 52 (John fragment, ~125 CE). These are included as speculative, comparative material only — not evidence for the core argument.
